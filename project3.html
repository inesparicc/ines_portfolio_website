OBBI: Bias Mapping Tool

A web app that helps teams compare themselves with their target audience and spot possible gaps in a clear, data-driven way.

Type

Academic group project · 15 weeks

Role

UX research · UI design · testing

Team

1 designer · 2 developers

Tools

Figma · Miro · React · TypeScript · .NET

The challenge

Help teams discuss bias without blaming individuals.

OBBI started from the question of how unconscious bias could be mapped in a way that feels objective, approachable, and useful.

My role was to translate research and expert feedback into a product concept, UX flow, visual design, and testing approach.

Context Research Concept Design Build Testing Outcome
01

Context

From unconscious bias to objective gap mapping

The project started as an HR unconscious bias tool. Through research and expert feedback, the team learned that bias is sensitive to measure directly and can easily feel judgmental.

We shifted toward a metrics-based approach that compares a team with its target audience and highlights gaps as conversation starters.

02

Research

Understanding bias with experts

I interviewed HR professionals and a psychology expert to understand how bias appears at work, what makes bias tools difficult to trust, and how to keep the experience low-threshold.

We also researched existing tools like Harvard's IAT and bias interrupter playbooks to understand what already exists and where our concept could be different.

Research insight

People need a safe way to discuss bias without feeling labelled.

Design goal

Make the tool objective, approachable, and easy to understand.

03

Concept

Pivoting from scenarios to metrics

We explored two concepts: a scenario-based game and a metrics-based gap analysis. Users enjoyed the scenarios, but stakeholders preferred the metrics concept because it felt more objective and scalable.

I helped reshape the direction into OBBI: a web app that compares team and audience data, then explains possible gaps in plain language.

04

Design

Designing a calm tool for a sensitive topic

The visual design needed to feel friendly and non-judgmental. I worked on flows, wireframes, high-fidelity screens, and chart readability.

Testing showed that users preferred bar charts over circular charts because they were easier to read and compare.

Plain language

Results needed explanation, not only numbers.

Low pressure

The interface should support reflection, not blame.

Readable data

Bar charts made differences easier to understand.

05

Build

Collaborating with developers

I worked with two software developers to keep the design aligned with the technical architecture. We used user stories, GitLab issues, and weekly check-ins to track progress.

I also implemented a small React demo modal component used during demos, which gave me the chance to apply my UI decisions directly in code.

06

Testing

Testing clarity, charts, and concept direction

I created a testing plan to compare concepts and review the visual design with HR professionals, students, and design experts.

Feedback led to simpler input, more whitespace, clearer charts, and a calmer visual structure.

  • Bar charts were easier to read than circular charts.
  • Age ranges felt more comfortable than exact ages.
  • Users needed explanations alongside data visualisations.
  • The tool worked best when it framed gaps as discussion starters.

Outcome

A supportive bias-mapping tool that turns data gaps into team reflection.

This project taught me how to balance research, ethics, design, and development in one product.

I learned how to handle a pivot in scope, design respectful data visualisations, and collaborate with developers while keeping the end-user experience simple.
